The collection

About 1,000 records, owned by the bar

The shelves are the bar's own — roughly 1,000 records spanning jazz, old-school hip-hop, rock 'n' roll, indie rock and electronica. Nights are built by hand, the way a crate is dug: a 1970s rock night might put Bob Seger on one turntable and Tom Petty on the other.

Records are chosen to be heard whole. The deep cut after the single is the reason the single exists.

The ritual

Two records in 90 minutes, silence included

A 90-minute visit could include two complete records — deep tracks and some dead air. Between sides the room goes quiet on purpose. It is the pause that makes the next record land.

The etiquette is simple and strictly enforced by the room itself: phones stay in pockets, the music finishes the side, and the conversation happens at a volume the record can win.

No shuffle, no phone glow: full records, dead air included. The premise · downtown Durham

The tuning

A room built to disappear behind the music

Sapele wood and black walnut, carpeted floors, sound panels and clouds on the walls and ceiling. The old pink-neon, tiki-era finish is gone; what replaced it is dark, cozy and moody, with softer lighting — a library for records, as design lead Nellie Vail intended.

Five tables and a row of barstools face the room. The lounge takes plush couches and swivel chairs. Acoustics first, always: the panels are not decoration.
